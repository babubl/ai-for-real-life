What is AI, and where did it come from?

Intelligence, rules versus learning, and 75 years in one list.

Artificial Intelligence is the science of making machines do things that need intelligence when people do them: recognising a face, understanding speech, translating, planning a route, making a decision, writing a letter.

Two ways to make a machine smart

  • Rules (old AI, "expert systems"): humans write every rule. "If temperature above 100°F and cough, suggest flu." Works for narrow, predictable jobs. Breaks the moment reality doesn't match a rule.
  • Learning (modern AI): show the machine thousands of examples and let it work out the rules itself. This is machine learning, and it's behind almost everything called AI today.
Example: Writing rules to recognise a cat is nearly impossible (ears? some cats fold them; fur? some are hairless). Show a machine a million cat photos and it learns "cat" better than any rulebook.

Narrow AI vs general AI

Narrow AI is good at one job: Google Maps can't write poems, and a chess engine can't drive. Even ChatGPT, broad as it seems, is a language tool with clear limits. AGI (Artificial General Intelligence) means AI that can learn any intellectual task a human can. It doesn't exist yet; experts strongly disagree on when, or whether, it will.

Test: Is a calculator AI? Is Google Maps traffic prediction AI?

A calculator follows fixed rules written by people and never learns, so it isn't AI in the modern sense. Maps traffic prediction learned patterns from millions of past journeys, so it is machine learning, a kind of AI.

Go deeper3 resources · about 3 hours

Researchers describe AI with two properties: autonomy, performing tasks in complex environments without constant guidance, and adaptivity, improving by learning from experience. A thermostat has a little autonomy but no adaptivity; a spam filter that learns from your clicks has both. University courses also distinguish four ways of defining AI: thinking like humans, acting like humans (the Turing test), thinking rationally, and acting rationally. Most modern AI aims at the last: acting to get the best expected outcome.

How AI solves problems: search and planning

Before machines learned from data, they searched. They still do, every time Google Maps finds your route.

Many problems can be described as finding a path through possible choices. To get from Chennai to Madurai, a navigation app looks at a map as a network of junctions (called states) joined by roads (actions), each with a cost such as minutes or kilometres. The goal is the cheapest path from start to destination.

Blind search vs smart search

  • Blind (uninformed) search tries options in a fixed order, like checking every road out of Chennai one by one. It works, but it's painfully slow on a big map.
  • Heuristic search uses a good guess to decide what to try first. The famous A* ("A-star") algorithm adds the distance travelled so far to a straight-line estimate of the distance left, so it heads towards Madurai instead of wandering towards Nellore.

Games: thinking about the opponent

In chess, you can't just find a path: your opponent moves too. Game-playing programs use minimax: assume the opponent always plays their best move, and choose the move that leaves you best off in the worst case. The number of possible chess games is astronomically large, so programs look only a few moves ahead and use a score to judge positions. This is how Deep Blue beat Kasparov in 1997.

Optimisation: finding the best arrangement

Some problems aren't about a path but the best combination: a school timetable with no clashes, delivery routes for 40 vans, a factory shift roster. AI tries an arrangement, measures how good it is, makes a small change, and keeps it if it's better. Picture climbing a hill in fog: each step goes uphill, but you might stop on a small hill and miss the mountain. Smarter methods add random jumps to escape these traps.

Why this still matters: today's AI agents combine both worlds. An LLM proposes steps, and search-style planning decides which step to try next, checks the result and backtracks when something fails.
Test: Why does A* reach the answer faster than trying every road?

It uses a heuristic, a sensible estimate of the distance remaining, to try promising roads first and ignore ones that lead the wrong way.

Data: the food AI eats

No data, no learning. Bad data, bad learning.

If machine learning is cooking, data is the ingredients. A model can only be as good as the examples it learned from.

Kinds of data

  • Structured: neat rows and columns, like a bank statement or an Excel sheet of sales.
  • Unstructured: everything messy: photos, voice notes, emails, videos, WhatsApp chats. Most of the world's data is like this, and deep learning is what finally made it usable.

Words you'll hear

  • Dataset: the collection of examples, e.g. 50,000 photos of mangoes.
  • Features: the details the model looks at, e.g. colour, size, softness.
  • Label: the right answer attached to an example, e.g. "ripe" or "not ripe". Humans often add labels by hand; that's a real job.
  • Training data / test data: you learn from one part and check yourself on another part you've never seen (lesson 4).

Garbage in, garbage out

Train a face-unlock system mostly on light-skinned faces and it works worse on darker skin. Train a hiring tool on 10 years of past hiring where mostly men were picked and it learns to prefer men. This is bias: the AI isn't evil, it faithfully copies patterns in its data, including unfair ones.

Test: You want AI to spot spoiled tomatoes at a market. What data do you need?

Lots of photos of tomatoes, each labelled "fresh" or "spoiled", from different lighting, angles, varieties and phones, so it doesn't just learn one stall's lighting. Keep some photos aside for testing.

Professionals spend most of their time on data. Look up data cleaning (missing values, duplicates, wrong units), class imbalance (when one label is rare, like fraud), data drift (the world changes after training), and data provenance (where data came from and whether there was consent). Under India's Digital Personal Data Protection Act, consent and purpose matter when personal data is used.

Probability: how AI handles uncertainty

AI almost never knows for sure. It deals in chances, and so should you when you read its answers.

The real world is messy, so AI systems express beliefs as probabilities: "70% chance of rain", "98% likely spam", "this transaction looks unusual". Even ChatGPT works this way: it picks each next word from a list of likely options (you played with this in Week 2).

The trap: ignoring the base rate

Suppose a disease affects 1 in 1,000 people, and a test catches 99% of real cases but also wrongly flags 1% of healthy people. You test positive. What's the chance you have the disease? Most people say 99%. The real answer is about 9%. Out of 1,000 people, about 1 is sick and tests positive, but about 10 healthy people also test positive. So only 1 in 11 positives is real.

This is Bayes' rule: update what you believed before (the base rate) with the new evidence. Try it in the calculator. Change how rare the condition is and watch the answer move.

Bayes at work: the spam filter

Early spam filters, called naive Bayes filters, learned how often each word appears in spam versus normal mail. "Lottery", "KYC update" and "click here" push the odds towards spam; your manager's name pushes them back. Multiply the evidence from every word and you get a probability.

What this means for you: a fraud alert, a medical AI flag or a CV-screening score is a probability, not a verdict. When the thing being detected is rare, most alarms are false alarms, even from a very accurate system.
Test: A fraud detector is 99% accurate, but only 1 in 10,000 transactions is fraud. Most of its alerts will be…?

False alarms. Fraud is so rare that the 1% of genuine transactions it wrongly flags vastly outnumbers the real fraud it catches.

Go deeper3 resources · about 4 hours

Next steps: conditional probability (the chance of A given B), the full form of Bayes' rule, Bayesian networks (maps of how causes and effects relate), and Markov models (predicting sequences, the ancestor of next-word prediction). These underpin weather forecasts, speech recognition and medical decision tools.

Machine learning: three ways to learn

With a teacher, without a teacher, and by trial and error.

1. Supervised learning: learning with answers

Like a student with a guidebook full of solved problems. Every example comes with the right answer (label). Two flavours:

  • Classification: pick a category. Spam or not spam? Fraud or genuine? Which digit is this?
  • Regression: predict a number. What will this flat rent for? How many units will sell in December?

2. Unsupervised learning: finding patterns alone

No answers given. The machine groups things that look similar. A supermarket finds that its customers fall into 5 natural groups ("weekend bulk buyers", "late-night snackers") without anyone defining them. This is called clustering.

3. Reinforcement learning: trial, error and rewards

Like training a puppy with biscuits. The machine tries actions, gets rewards or penalties, and slowly learns what works. AlphaGo learned Go by playing millions of games against itself. ChatGPT and Claude were also polished this way, with humans rating answers (called RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback).

Test: Which type is each? (a) predicting house prices from past sales (b) grouping songs by mood with no labels (c) a robot learning to walk

(a) supervised, regression; (b) unsupervised, clustering; (c) reinforcement learning.

Go deeper3 resources · about 5 hours

Look into the main model families: linear and logistic regression, decision trees and random forests (popular in banking because they are easier to explain), k-nearest neighbours, support vector machines and k-means clustering. Also self-supervised learning, where the data labels itself, which is how LLMs learn from raw text.

Training and testing: how a machine actually learns

Guess, measure the mistake, adjust a little, repeat. Watch it happen.

Every learning machine runs the same loop, thousands or billions of times:

  1. Guess using its current settings (weights).
  2. Check the guess against the right answer. The size of the error is called the loss.
  3. Adjust the weights a tiny bit in the direction that reduces the error.
  4. Repeat for every example, many rounds (called epochs).

Try it in the experiment. Each dot is a mango: across is colour (green to yellow), up is softness. Pink dots are ripe. The line is the model's current "ripe vs not ripe" boundary. It starts out bad. Press Train and watch it learn.

Memorising vs understanding

A student who memorises last year's answers scores 100% on last year's paper and fails the new one. A model can do the same: perfect on its training data, useless on new data. That's overfitting. The cure: hide some data during training (the test set) and judge the model only on that.

Real-world numbers: a model that's 99% accurate sounds great, but if only 1 in 100 transactions is fraud, a model that always says "not fraud" is also 99% accurate and catches nothing. Always ask: accurate at what, tested on what? Lesson 7 shows how professionals measure this.
Test: Your model scores 98% on training data and 61% on test data. What happened?

Overfitting. It memorised the training examples instead of learning the general pattern. Fixes: more varied data, a simpler model, or stopping training earlier.

Go deeper3 resources · about 3 hours

The engine of training is gradient descent: measure the error, work out which direction to nudge each weight to reduce it, take a small step, repeat. The size of the step is the learning rate. Learn about underfitting (too simple to capture the pattern), regularisation (penalising complexity to prevent overfitting) and cross-validation.

Measuring a model: why accuracy isn't enough

How professionals judge whether an AI is any good, and the trade-off hiding behind every alert.

Take a fraud detector. Every prediction lands in one of four boxes, called the confusion matrix:

  • True positive: it said fraud, and it was fraud.
  • False positive: it said fraud, but it was genuine (an annoyed customer).
  • False negative: it said genuine, but it was fraud (money lost).
  • True negative: it said genuine, and it was.

Two questions that matter more than accuracy

  • Precision: when it raises an alarm, how often is it right? Low precision means many false alarms.
  • Recall: of all the real fraud, how much did it catch? Low recall means fraud slips through.

You usually can't max out both. Most models give each case a score, and someone chooses a threshold: flag everything above it. Lower the threshold and you catch more fraud (higher recall) but annoy more customers (lower precision). Move the slider in the experiment to feel this trade-off.

Which matters more depends on the cost of each mistake. A cancer screening test should favour recall: missing a case is worse than a follow-up test. A spam filter should favour precision: losing a real email is worse than seeing one spam message.

Training, validation and test data

Professionals split data three ways: training data to learn from, validation data to tune settings such as the threshold, and test data, touched only once at the end, to report honest results.

How LLMs are measured

Chatbots are scored on benchmarks (standard exam-style question sets) and by human raters comparing answers side by side. Benchmarks help, but a model can score well on a test it has partly seen during training, so always try a tool on your own real tasks.

Test: A disease screening AI should usually favour precision or recall?

Recall. Missing a real case is costly; a false alarm leads to a follow-up test.

Go deeper3 resources · about 3 hours

Professionals also use the F1 score (a balance of precision and recall), the ROC curve (how precision and recall trade off across every threshold) and calibration (whether a "70% sure" prediction is right 70% of the time). For fairness, they compare these numbers across groups of people, such as women and men or urban and rural applicants.

Neural networks and deep learning

A neuron is just a weighted vote. Stack millions of them and you get something remarkable.

One artificial neuron

A neuron takes some inputs, multiplies each by a weight (how much it matters), adds them up with a starting nudge called the bias, and turns the total into an output. That's all. Try the experiment: should I go for an evening walk?

Change the inputs to see the decision change. Then change the weights: that's exactly what training does, automatically, across billions of weights.

From one neuron to deep learning

A neural network connects neurons in layers: each layer's outputs become the next layer's inputs. Deep learning just means many layers. The magic is that each layer learns to spot more complex things on its own, without anyone telling it what to look for:

Layer 1edges and colours
Layer 2curves, corners, textures
Layer 3eyes, wheels, petals
Final layers"this is a cat"

How does it learn? Through backpropagation: after a wrong answer, the error is passed backwards through the layers, and every weight gets a tiny share of the blame and a tiny adjustment. Repeat over millions of examples.

Why now? The ideas are from the 1980s. They took off after 2012 because of three things together: huge data (the internet), cheap powerful GPUs, and better training tricks.

Test: What makes deep learning "deep"?

The number of layers in the neural network. More layers let it learn more complex patterns, building from simple features to complex ones.

Go deeper3 resources · about 6 hours

Different jobs use different network designs. Convolutional neural networks (CNNs) slide small filters across images and power face unlock and medical imaging. Recurrent neural networks (RNNs) processed sequences such as speech before Transformers replaced them. Learn what an activation function does and why depth helps networks build complex features from simple ones.

From deep learning to generative AI and LLMs

How we got from "is this a cat?" to "write my leave letter".

Older AI was mostly discriminative: it sorted and judged things (spam or not, cat or dog). Generative AI creates new things that look like its training data: new sentences, new pictures, new voices.

The Transformer and "attention"

In 2017, a design called the Transformer changed everything. Its key idea is attention: when reading a word, the model looks at every other word and decides which ones matter for its meaning. In "The bank of the river was muddy", attention links "bank" to "river", so it knows this isn't a bank with an ATM.

How an LLM is made: three stages

  1. Pre-training: read a huge slice of the internet, books and code, and practise one game over and over: predict the next word. This takes thousands of GPUs for months and produces a model that knows language and a lot about the world.
  2. Fine-tuning: train further on examples of good conversations, so it behaves like a helpful assistant rather than an autocomplete.
  3. Human feedback (RLHF): people rate answers; the model is nudged towards helpful, honest, safe ones.

Image and video generators mostly use a different trick called diffusion: start from random noise and remove it step by step until an image matching your words appears.

The full inside story of how ChatGPT and Claude answer you, with tokens, weights and parameters, is Week 2 of the course.

Test: What is the one game an LLM practises during pre-training?

Predicting the next word (more precisely, the next token) in a piece of text. Everything else it can do grows out of getting very good at that.

Go deeper3 resources · about 2–15 hours

Two ideas unlock how LLMs work. Embeddings turn each word into a long list of numbers, so words with similar meanings sit near each other ("king" near "queen", "Chennai" near "Madras"). Attention lets each word look at every other word to work out its meaning in context. Stack dozens of attention layers, train on trillions of words, then fine-tune with human feedback, and you get a chat assistant.

How AI projects are built

The project cycle taught in Indian schools and used by real AI teams: from a problem to a working system.

Behind every AI feature is a project that follows roughly the same cycle. India's CBSE AI curriculum calls it the AI project cycle; industry teams use almost the same steps.

  1. Problem scoping. Who has the problem, what exactly is it, where does it happen, and why does solving it matter? Write a one-line goal you can measure.
  2. Data acquisition. What examples exist, who owns them, is there consent, and are they representative?
  3. Data exploration. Look at the data: charts, gaps, errors, imbalances. Most project time goes here.
  4. Modelling. Try simple approaches first, then more complex ones. Sometimes a rule or a spreadsheet beats AI.
  5. Evaluation. Measure on unseen test data with the right metric (precision, recall, cost of mistakes).
  6. Deployment and monitoring. Put it in front of real users, watch for drift as the world changes, and keep a human fallback.

Choosing a good AI project

In AI for Everyone, Andrew Ng suggests looking for the overlap of what AI can do and what is valuable to the business. A rough rule of thumb from his course: if a person can do the task with about a second of thought (is this a cat, is this signature smudged), supervised learning can often do it too, given enough data. Tasks needing deep judgement, rare data or complex reasoning are much harder.

Who's on an AI team

  • Domain expert: knows the problem (the doctor, the loan officer, the farmer).
  • Data engineer: collects and cleans the data.
  • Machine learning engineer: builds and trains models.
  • Product manager: decides what to build and for whom.
  • Business analyst: turns the problem into clear requirements and success measures.
Test: Which step usually takes the most time in a real AI project?

Getting and exploring the data: collecting it, cleaning it, labelling it and understanding its gaps. Modelling is often the shorter part.

Limits, risks and ethics

Knowing what AI can't do is what makes you good at using it.

  • It doesn't "know", it predicts. It can sound sure and be wrong (hallucination). Verify facts that matter.
  • Bias: it repeats unfairness in its data. Matters most in hiring, loans, policing, healthcare.
  • Privacy: what you type may be stored. Never share secrets, IDs or other people's private details.
  • Deepfakes and scams: fake voices and videos are now cheap. See the family safety guide.
  • Copyright: who owns AI-generated work, and whether training on artists' work is fair, is being fought out in courts right now.
  • Jobs: AI changes tasks more than it removes whole jobs, so far. People who use AI well tend to replace people who don't. Economists genuinely disagree on the long-term effect.
  • Energy: training and running big models uses a lot of electricity and water for data centres.
  • Black box: deep learning models often can't explain why they decided something, which is a problem when a decision affects a person's life.

Principles companies and governments use

Most responsible-AI frameworks share the same core ideas. Microsoft's widely used list has six: fairness, reliability and safety, privacy and security, inclusiveness, transparency and accountability. UNESCO's framework for students adds a human-centred mindset: AI should support people's choices, not replace their judgement.

The law is catching up

  • India, personal data: the Digital Personal Data Protection Act was passed in August 2023, and its Rules were notified in November 2025, with obligations phased in over about 18 months. Companies need clear consent and a stated purpose to use your personal data.
  • India, AI: the India AI Governance Guidelines, released by MeitY in November 2025, take a principle-based approach built on "do no harm", relying on existing laws and sector regulators (such as RBI and SEBI) rather than one new AI law.
  • European Union: the EU AI Act entered into force in August 2024. It sorts AI by risk: some uses, such as social scoring, are banned outright, high-risk uses such as hiring and credit face strict rules, and most of its rules apply from August 2026.

Laws change. Check the official sources in "Go deeper" for the current position. This is general information, not legal advice.

Test: Name two situations where you should never rely on AI alone.

Any of: medical decisions, legal matters, big money moves, anything about another person's reputation, safety-critical instructions. Use AI to understand and prepare, then confirm with a qualified human or official source.
