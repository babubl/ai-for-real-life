Family AI safety

AI makes scams more convincing. Here's how to stay ahead.

A cloned voice, a fake video of a celebrity, a perfect-looking investment app. Ten minutes with this page can save someone you love a lot of money and worry. Read it, then share it with your parents first.

1930

Lost money to cyber fraud in India? Call the national cyber crime helpline 1930 immediately, or report at cybercrime.gov.in. The faster you report, the better the chance of freezing the money.

The "emergency" call in a familiar voice

Someone calls sounding exactly like your son, daughter or relative: crying, in trouble, needing money now. Or a "police officer" puts them on the line. Voices can be cloned from a few seconds of audio taken from a reel, a video or a voicemail.

What to do

  • Agree a family code word today: a word only your family knows, asked for in any money emergency.
  • Hang up and call back on the number saved in your phone, not the number that called.
  • Slow down. Scammers rely on panic and "don't tell anyone". Real emergencies survive a two-minute check.
  • Never pay by gift card, crypto or a new UPI ID because a caller insists.

Deepfake videos of famous people

A well-known business leader, actor or news anchor appears to recommend a trading app, a "government AI scheme" or a guaranteed-return investment. These are usually AI-generated deepfakes running as paid ads.

  • Real public figures don't promote get-rich schemes in social media ads.
  • Look for lips slightly out of sync, odd blinking, a flat voice, and a link to an unfamiliar website or WhatsApp number.
  • Search the person's name and the scheme on a news site before doing anything.

"AI trading bots" and fake advisers

Anyone promising fixed monthly profits from an "AI bot" or "algo" is running a scam. Real investments carry risk and never guarantee high returns.

  • Before trusting investment advice, check the person or firm is registered with SEBI using the intermediary search at sebi.gov.in.
  • Be wary of WhatsApp or Telegram "investment groups" that show screenshots of profits and ask you to install an app.
  • If you can't withdraw your money, or you're asked to pay a "tax" or "fee" to withdraw, stop and report it.

This is general safety information, not investment advice.

Fake AI apps and sites

Look-alike apps use names such as "ChatGPT Pro" or "Gemini AI Plus" to collect data or charge hidden subscriptions.

  • Install only from the official app store and check the publisher: OpenAI for ChatGPT, Anthropic for Claude, Google for Gemini, Microsoft for Copilot.
  • Use the official websites: chatgpt.com, claude.ai, gemini.google.com.
  • Check your app store subscriptions once a month.

What never to paste into any chatbot

  • OTPs, passwords and PINs
  • Aadhaar, PAN, passport or bank account numbers, card details
  • Other people's private information: medical reports, salary slips, phone numbers
  • Confidential work or client data, unless your employer allows it

If you need help with a document that contains these, cover or delete them first. A photo with the numbers covered works just as well.

Privacy settings worth two minutes

  • Training on your chats: ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini let you control whether your conversations are used to improve their models. Look under Settings → Data controls or Privacy.
  • Memory: assistants can remember facts about you across chats. Review what's saved, and delete anything you don't want kept.
  • Temporary or incognito chats aren't saved to your history.
  • Connected apps: if you link Gmail, Drive or Calendar, check what access you've given and remove what you don't use.

Menu names change often. If you can't find a setting, ask the assistant itself: "Where do I turn off training on my chats?"

Children and AI

  • Explain three things: AI can be wrong, it isn't a friend, and it should never get your photo, school or address.
  • Use it together for homework at first. A good rule: AI can explain and quiz, but the answer is written by the child.
  • Check age rules: most assistants require users to be 13 or older, some 18, and younger children need a parent account.
  • Talk about AI "companion" apps and filters that make fake images of real people. Making or sharing fake images of classmates causes real harm.

A 5-minute talk with older parents

Many voice and investment scams target people over 60. A short, calm conversation works better than a long lecture. You could say:

"Amma, Appa, these days phones can copy anyone's voice. If you ever get a call from me or anyone in the family asking for money urgently, ask for our code word, then hang up and call me back on my saved number. Even if it really sounds like me."

  1. Agree the code word together.
  2. Save 1930 in their phone as "Cyber Fraud Helpline".
  3. Show them how to block and report a number.
  4. Tell them they will never be scolded for checking with you first.

Checking photos, videos and forwards

  • Pause before forwarding anything shocking, especially just before elections, festivals or during a crisis.
  • Look for warped hands, melted text, odd shadows, and faces that look slightly too smooth.
  • Search for the claim on a trusted news site, or use Google Lens to find where the image first appeared.
  • "Forwarded many times" is not a source.

If it happens

  1. Call 1930 or report at cybercrime.gov.in straight away. Hours matter.
  2. Call your bank on the number on your card or the bank's official website to block cards and UPI.
  3. Change passwords for email and banking, and switch on two-step verification.
  4. Keep evidence: screenshots, phone numbers, UPI IDs, transaction references.
  5. Tell family. Scammers often try the same trick on relatives next.
